## H1

**Salesforce operations for teams that no longer trust the process**

### Subhead

Independent Salesforce operations consulting in the Toronto area, focused on the
point where business process, automation, reporting and day-to-day ownership
stop lining up.

The work starts with a practical question: what is making Salesforce harder to
operate than it should be?

---

## Your automation has become a risk

A Salesforce org can reach the point where routine changes are avoided because
nobody is certain what will happen next.

Flows exist, dependencies accumulate, and the safest answer becomes "do not
touch it."

That is an operations problem before it is a configuration problem.

The first task is to understand what the process is supposed to do, what
Salesforce is currently doing, where decisions are being made, and which areas
need closer inspection before anyone changes them.

## Your reporting is no longer trusted

A dashboard can run perfectly and still fail the business.

If people disagree about what the numbers mean, build their own spreadsheets, or
question whether records are complete, the issue is bigger than the report
itself.

The useful questions sit underneath it: how is information entering Salesforce,
who owns it, where does the process allow inconsistency, and what happens to the
data after it is entered?

Reliable reporting starts with understanding the operating process behind the
numbers.

## Your process and Salesforce no longer match

Business processes change. Salesforce often keeps the history.

Over time, users develop workarounds, fields outlive their original purpose,
steps get added without removing old ones, and the system begins reflecting
several versions of the same process.

The work here is to separate the process that exists today from the process
Salesforce was originally built around.

The consultant brings a background in Salesforce operations, Lean Six Sigma and
Scrum to that examination: understand the work, identify where it breaks down,
and establish what needs attention before adding more complexity.

## Nobody clearly owns what should happen next

Some Salesforce problems stay open because they are technical.

Others stay open because nobody has converted them into a decision.

There may be a backlog of fixes, competing requests, unclear priorities, or
several people who can describe the problem without anyone being responsible for
resolving it.

A useful engagement should make those decisions visible: what needs
investigation, what depends on something else, what carries risk, and what can
wait.

The objective is not to generate a longer backlog. It is to make the next
decision clearer.

## AI agents do the legwork. A human owns the decision.

SFDC24 is operated by one independent consultant.

AI agents are used for legwork within the delivery process. Anything
consequential is reviewed and signed off by the consultant before it becomes a
decision or action.

That arrangement is deliberate. The buyer does not need to understand or manage
a fleet of agents. The technology is there to support the work; accountability
stays with the person responsible for delivering it.

## My notes before this goes live

**What it gets right.** Four sections that each name a situation the buyer is
already in, in their language, with no adjectives. "A dashboard can run perfectly
and still fail the business" is the strongest line in it. The AI section handles
the hardest problem on the page — explaining the agent fleet without making the
buyer responsible for understanding it — by putting accountability first.

**What is missing, and it is the same gap as ever.** There is no proof. Four
problem statements and no evidence any of them were ever solved. That is honest,
because there is nothing publishable yet, but it caps how well this can convert.
The fix is `intake/` — one real anonymised engagement turns this from a
well-written page into a credible one.

**Word count is ~600**, at the top of the brief. That is right for a page with no
proof: it has to earn attention by being useful rather than by being short.

**One risk.** Every section is a problem statement. A skeptical reader may reach
the bottom having agreed four times without ever learning what actually gets
delivered. The diagnostic offer — fixed fee, written recommendation — belongs on
this page, not just in the intake flow.

### What the first piece of work looks like

Before anything gets changed, there is a diagnostic: a fixed piece of work that
ends in a written recommendation.

It starts with a conversation and a look at what you already have. How the
process is supposed to run, what the system is actually doing, and where the two
have come apart. Where a read-only look at the org is useful and you are
comfortable granting it, that helps; where you are not, the work runs on your
own descriptions, exports and screenshots. Usually a week or two, depending on
how many people need talking to.

What arrives at the end is a short document, not a slide deck. What is actually
wrong, ordered by what it is costing you. What should be done first, and why.
What to leave alone for now. Where a fix is straightforward it says so. Where it
needs a decision from someone with authority, it names the decision rather than
burying it in a backlog.

It commits you to nothing. If the recommendation is worth acting on and you want
it done, that is a separate conversation. If it is not, you still have the
document.
