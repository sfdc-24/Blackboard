Conversations are saved. A human reads them.

You've met this org before

Someone built it in 2019. Then someone else built on top of that. The documentation is a text field called Notes__c, the sandbox hasn't matched production since the last refresh nobody remembers doing, and there's a validation rule everyone routes around but no one will delete in case it's load-bearing.

Nothing here is broken enough to be an emergency. It's just heavier than it should be, every single week.

Four ways that shows up

The flows have grown teeth

Routine changes get avoided because nobody's certain what fires next. Eleven decision nodes, no descriptions, and one of them was definitely a Friday afternoon. The safest answer becomes "don't touch it" — and that's an operations problem long before it's a configuration one.

Nobody trusts the numbers

A dashboard can run perfectly and still fail the business. When people build their own spreadsheets rather than open the report, the report isn't the problem. What's underneath it is: how data arrives, who owns it, and where the process quietly allows two right answers.

The process moved on without telling Salesforce

The business changed. The org kept the receipts. Fields outlive their purpose, steps get added without old ones coming out, and eventually you're supporting three versions of the same process at once — two of which nobody has done since 2023.

Nobody owns what happens next

Some problems stay open because they're hard. Others stay open because nobody turned them into a decision. There's a backlog, competing requests, and four people who can describe the issue perfectly and none who can approve fixing it.

What the first piece of work looks like

Before anything gets changed, there's a diagnostic: a fixed piece of work that ends in a written recommendation.

It starts with a conversation and a look at what you already have. How the process is meant to run, what the system is actually doing, and where the two came apart. A read-only look at the org helps if you're comfortable granting it; if you're not, it runs on your own descriptions, exports and screenshots. Usually a week or two, depending on how many people need talking to.

What arrives at the end is a short document, not a slide deck. What's actually wrong, ordered by what it's costing you. What to do first, and why. What to leave alone. Where a fix is straightforward it says so; where it needs a decision from someone with authority, it names the decision instead of burying it in a backlog.

It commits you to nothing. If it's worth acting on and you want it done, that's a separate conversation. If it isn't, you still have the document.

Who you're actually dealing with

One independent consultant in the Toronto area, with a background in Salesforce operations, Lean Six Sigma and Scrum. Not an agency, not a practice, not a bench.

AI agents do the legwork — reading, cross-checking, drafting, keeping track. Anything consequential is reviewed and signed off by a human before it becomes a decision or an action. That arrangement is deliberate: you shouldn't have to understand or manage a fleet of agents to get value from one. The technology is here to do the tedious part. Accountability stays with the person doing the work.
